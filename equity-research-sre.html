Business Model & Segment Breakdown

Sempra is three businesses under one holding company, and pricing it as a plain regulated utility misses the point in both directions. Two legs are classic regulated rate-base compounding. The third — LNG export infrastructure — is a contracted, commodity-adjacent growth business that no other name in the XLU top 10 owns at material scale.

Geographic distribution

  • California: regulated electric and gas distribution — the largest political and regulatory exposure, including wildfire liability frameworks (§4).
  • Texas: Oncor's T&D territory — the fastest underlying load-growth market in the portfolio, regulated at state level in a structurally different (wires-only, competitive retail) market design.
  • US Gulf Coast + Mexico: LNG export terminals on the Gulf Coast selling into global (Europe / Asia) demand, plus Mexico energy infrastructure — the only internationally exposed earnings in the group, and the source of both the premium and the geopolitical risk.

Recent structural pivots

  • From pure utility toward utility-plus-infrastructure: the centre of investment gravity has shifted toward LNG liquefaction capacity (Port Arthur build-out, Cameron expansion optionality) alongside — not instead of — regulated rate-base growth in California and Texas.
  • Texas as the hedge: Oncor's growth deliberately balances a California book whose returns are increasingly contested politically. Two large, dissimilar regulatory regimes is the portfolio construction inside the company itself.
  • No merchant-generation pivot: unlike Constellation's merchant nuclear economics, Sempra's non-regulated exposure runs through contracted infrastructure fees — lower upside torque than merchant power, materially lower downside torque too.

Supply-chain & dependency notes

  • LNG: liquefaction trains depend on specialised EPC contractors, cryogenic equipment and long-lead components shared with every other Gulf Coast export project — construction timelines, not demand, are the near-term constraint on Phase 2 value. Feed-gas supply and pipeline access into the terminals are the operating dependency.
  • Regulated grids (CA + TX): as across the sector, large transformers, switchgear and skilled labour gate the pace at which approved capex becomes rate base. (Industry-level context; the pack carries no Sempra-specific procurement figures, so none are quantified here.)
  • Gas distribution (SoCalGas): California's long-run decarbonisation policy is a structural demand question for the gas network specifically — a slow-moving, but real, dependency on state energy policy that the electric and Texas legs do not share.

03

Competitive Moat & Industry Positioning

Moat rating: wide regulated franchises, plus a contracted-infrastructure moat that peers cannot copy quickly. Sempra's distinctiveness is the combination: most utilities own one regulatory regime's worth of risk. Sempra owns two large, dissimilar ones — California and Texas — and bolts on LNG liquefaction capacity that took a decade of permitting and construction to create. Each leg's moat is a different kind of barrier.

Moat strengths

  • Two-state regulatory diversification: California (CPUC) and Texas (Oncor) earnings answer to different regulators, different market designs and different politics. A bad outcome in Sacramento does not impair Austin — the pack's central moat point, and the direct answer to Sempra's own biggest risk (§4).
  • Exclusive regulated franchises: SDG&E, SoCalGas and Oncor operate natural-monopoly service territories. Demand is grown with the territory, not won from a rival utility.
  • LNG barriers to entry are time, permits and capital: Cameron and Port Arthur sit on permitted Gulf Coast sites with liquefaction capacity in place or under construction. A competitor cannot replicate that position quickly at any price — new US export capacity faces multi-year permitting and construction lead times.
  • Contracted LNG cash flows: long-term, largely take-or-pay liquefaction contracts (pack context — verify tenor before acting) convert a commodity-adjacent asset into something closer to infrastructure fees, dampening spot gas-price exposure on the contracted portion.
  • Texas load growth as a structural tailwind: Oncor's territory growth — population, industry, data centres — compounds rate base without Sempra having to win market share.

Moat limits & competitors

  • No pricing power where it matters most: regulated returns are granted by commissions, not taken in a market. California in particular has shown willingness to contest recovery — the moat protects the franchise, not the return on it.
  • Named utility competitors: Duke Energy (DUK) and Southern Company (SO) are the pack's peer set — larger regulated bases competing for the same utility investor dollar, generally at lower trailing multiples, which is the standing valuation challenge to SRE.
  • LNG competition is global and sector-crossing: the relevant LNG rival is Cheniere Energy — an Energy-sector name, not a Utilities peer — plus global export capacity. Sempra's liquefaction edge is real but contested by specialists whose entire business is this one asset class.
  • Wildfire liability can pierce the moat: California's wildfire framework can impose costs that no franchise exclusivity protects against — a liability regime, not a competitive one, is the sharpest threat to the California leg's economics.
  • Gas-network transition risk: SoCalGas's distribution moat assumes continued gas demand in a state actively legislating electrification — a slow erosion risk unique to this leg.

Positioning verdict: #7 by size, #1 by business-mix distinctiveness. Sempra is the top-10 utility an investor buys specifically to own LNG-export leverage with a regulated floor under it — and the 24.85× trailing multiple (highest in the regulated group ex-ETR) says the market already knows it. There is no “undiscovered” discount here; the question is purely whether the contracted growth arrives fast enough to justify a premium that peers do not command.

04

Catalysts & Downside Risks

Catalysts

  • LNG Phase 2 / export expansion — Cameron expansion and Port Arthur progress: each final-investment-decision, construction milestone or new long-term contract converts “optionality” into contracted earnings, which is exactly what the premium multiple needs to see.
  • Texas growth (Oncor) — continued large-load and population-driven rate-base growth in Texas is the lowest-drama catalyst: contracted by demography, diversified away from California politics.
  • California wildfire-cost recovery frameworks — constructive CPUC / legislative outcomes on how wildfire mitigation and liability costs are recovered would de-risk the California leg's earnings quality and could compress the risk premium embedded in the stock.
  • Global LNG demand (Europe / Asia) — sustained import demand in Europe and Asia supports both utilisation economics and the value of expansion capacity and re-contracting.

Downside risks

  • California wildfire liability & politics — the lead risk. Liability exposure, contested cost recovery, and a politically charged CPUC process can each hit earnings, cash flow or the multiple — and adverse precedents tend to arrive as step-changes, not drifts.
  • LNG global price / geopolitical exposure — the contracted book dampens spot exposure, but expansion value, re-contracting and Mexico infrastructure earnings remain tied to global gas markets and to Europe/Asia demand politics. A global LNG supply wave or demand shock reprices the growth leg first.
  • Multiple compression — at 24.85× trailing (highest in the regulated group ex-ETR), Sempra has the furthest to fall if utility multiples normalise or if LNG growth disappoints. The §5 bear cases are primarily multiple stories (17×), not earnings-collapse stories — that is the shape of the risk.
  • Execution across three build programmes — California grid work, Texas T&D and Gulf Coast liquefaction compete for capital, contractors and management attention simultaneously; delays push earnings to the right while the premium multiple stays in the present.
  • Rate & funding sensitivity — capital-intensive utilities carry refinancing exposure as a class; Sempra's specific leverage is unverified in this pack (§2), so treat the size of this risk as an open item rather than a quantified one.
What would break the thesis

A California wildfire-liability outcome that lands materially on shareholders without a credible recovery framework, arriving while LNG expansion slips. Either alone is absorbable — Texas and the regulated base carry one bad leg. Together they remove both the “regulated floor” and the “growth premium” justifications at once, and the stock's anchor becomes the bear-case 17× on a slower EPS path: $68–$74 territory in the §5 models.

Why the 5-year weighted price ($87.40) sits below the 12–18-month base target ($98) — explained, not hidden

The independent verification pass flagged exactly this: Sempra is one of three utilities whose 5-year weighted price falls below its 12–18-month base target. It is a plausibility flag, not an arithmetic error, and the mechanism is visible in the table above. The 12–18-month base ($98) assumes the market keeps paying today's premium — roughly 24–28× current earnings. The 5-year model assumes it does not: every case's frozen exit P/E (17× / 20× / 22×) sits below today's 24.85× trailing, so five years of 3–6.5% EPS compounding is mostly or entirely consumed by multiple normalisation. In the base case, EPS grows ~28% cumulatively while the multiple falls ~20% — the two nearly cancel, leaving $88.40. Read the pair of numbers together as the report's central tension: near term, Sempra is a “premium holds” trade; long term, on these frozen parameters, it is an earnings-delivery story whose price return is thin unless the bull case (LNG-led 6.5% growth at a still-premium 22×) is the one that happens — plus whatever dividend income (unverified here) accrues along the way. If you believe the LNG premium multiple persists at 24×+ for five years, your 5-year value is materially above $87.40 — but that is a different assumption, not this model.

SRE — Customer base (B2C-like note — no top-7 business-customer list)

Sempra sells gas and electricity principally to households and small businesses through California utilities (SDG&E, SoCalGas) and Texas (Oncor, ~80% owned), alongside LNG export volumes sold under long-term contracts, so it is treated as B2C-like for this task and no top-7 business-customer list is given. The pack names no LNG offtaker or large customer with a revenue share.

Key demand drivers: California and Texas regulated rate-base growth, LNG export contracting and global LNG demand, and California wildfire-cost recovery frameworks.

SRE — AI trade positioning

Role: Beneficiary (moderate). Sempra's AI exposure is indirect: Texas growth through Oncor (~80% owned) benefits from data-centre load in Texas, and its LNG export business benefits from global energy demand, but the pack names no AI-specific contract or load figure for Sempra.

While the AI trade runs — demand channel: Texas data-centre and industrial load growth lifts Oncor's regulated earnings, while LNG liquefaction contracts (largely take-or-pay per pack context) provide contracted export volumes that are resilient to short-term power-market swings.

While the AI trade runs — multiple channel: Sempra already trades at the highest trailing P/E in the regulated group ex-ETR (24.85 per pack), so the multiple embeds LNG and Texas optionality rather than a pure AI premium; an AI boom supports it only modestly.

When the AI trade unwinds — what breaks, in order: First, demand: Texas data-centre load slows or LNG demand softens on European/Asian weakness, hitting the two growth legs in turn. Second, multiple compression: from the group's highest trailing multiple toward peers if LNG Phase 2 or Texas growth disappoints. Third, balance sheet: TTM FCF is -$5.24B and California wildfire liability remains an overhang, so a higher cost of capital compounds both risks. No quantified sensitivity is claimed.

Trigger(s) to watch: Oncor load-growth data and Texas interconnection queues; LNG contracting and global gas-price trends; and California wildfire-cost recovery decisions.
