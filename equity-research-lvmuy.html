01 Business model & segment breakdown

LVMH is a portfolio of 75+ luxury houses sold through largely owned retail, priced for scarcity rather than volume. Five groups carry the economics; one of them carries most of the profit.

Fashion & Leather Goods — the profit engine

Louis Vuitton, Christian Dior Couture, Fendi, Loewe, Celine and others. The group's largest and most profitable division; its pricing power sets the tone for the whole portfolio.

Wines & Spirits

Moët & Chandon, Hennessy, Veuve Clicquot, Dom Pérignon. Prestige champagne and cognac — highly profitable, but the most cyclical and China / US-duty exposed part of the group.

Perfumes & Cosmetics

Dior Beauty, Guerlain, Givenchy Parfums. The most accessible entry price point into the brands — a recruitment funnel for younger luxury customers.

Watches & Jewellery

Tiffany & Co., Bulgari, TAG Heuer, Hublot. Tiffany's turnaround under LVMH ownership is a central part of the current equity story.

Selective Retailing

Sephora and DFS (travel retail). Sephora is the group's clearest structural growth asset; DFS remains tied to Asian travel flows.

Geography

Global, with Asia, the United States and Europe as the three decisive regions. Asia — above all the Chinese consumer, at home and travelling — is the swing factor for growth.

Recent structural pivots

Pricing power over volume: LVMH has leaned on price rises and mix rather than unit growth — sustainable while brand heat holds, a headwind when aspirational customers pull back. Sephora growth: continued rollout and share gains make beauty retail the diversification leg. Tiffany turnaround: repositioning the jeweller upmarket is still being proven. China recovery: the investment case now hinges less on new categories than on the Chinese and US luxury consumer returning.

Supply-chain note: vertical integration — owned ateliers, tanneries and vineyards — is itself part of the model: it secures scarce inputs (leather, grapes, craftsmanship) that rivals cannot easily buy. Segment-level revenue and profit splits were not re-verified in this research run, so none are quoted as figures here.

03 Competitive moat & industry positioning

The strongest moat in luxury — not one brand, but a system rivals cannot replicate at any realistic cost.

WHY THE MOAT IS WIDE

  • Brand heritage & scarcity: Louis Vuitton and Dior carry decades of accumulated desire; supply is deliberately constrained, protecting price.
  • Pricing power: repeated price rises have historically been absorbed without volume collapse — the defining luxury test.
  • Vertical retail: owned stores control presentation, client data and margin, rather than renting shelf space from department stores.
  • Portfolio breadth: weakness in one house or category (e.g. cognac) can be offset by strength elsewhere (e.g. Sephora, leather goods).

PRIMARY COMPETITORS

  • Hermès — purer scarcity, richer multiple; the quality benchmark.
  • Richemont — hard luxury (Cartier) strength in jewellery and watches.
  • Kering — Gucci-led; more concentrated, more volatile turnaround story.
  • Chanel — private; competes fiercely for the same client without public-market pressure.

LVMH's positioning trade-off is explicit: it is the most diversified luxury group, which dampens both the downside of any single brand cooling and the upside scarcity premium the market awards Hermès. The ~17.6× P/E is where that trade-off currently clears.

04 Catalysts & downside risks

CATALYSTS

  • China / US luxury recovery — any sustained return of the Chinese consumer and US aspirational spending re-rates the whole group.
  • Sephora momentum — continued growth in beauty retail, the least cyclical leg.
  • Further price rises — demonstrated pricing power dropping largely to margin.
  • Tiffany turnaround delivery — proof that the largest acquisition is earning its cost of capital.

DOWNSIDE RISKS

  • Luxury cyclicality — LVMH is discretionary spending at the highest ticket sizes; downturns hit volumes and mix together.
  • China consumer — a prolonged Chinese slowdown removes the sector's main growth engine.
  • Tariffs — US duties on European luxury goods would pressure Wines & Spirits first, then broader pricing.
  • FX — euro strength against the dollar and yen squeezes translated earnings; ADR holders carry a second FX layer.
  • Fashion risk — creative-director transitions and brand heat are inherently perishable, even at Louis Vuitton's scale.

AI trade positioning — LVMUY

Role: Low exposure. LVMH's AI exposure is marketing, clienteling and supply-chain efficiency, not infrastructure: the pack's thesis is pricing power, Sephora growth and China recovery, with FCF of €14.5bn in the latest year.

While the AI trade runs. While the AI trade runs, demand grows only indirectly, through better client targeting and e-commerce conversion; the multiple is supported by luxury pricing power, not an AI premium.

When the AI trade unwinds. If the trade unwinds, demand breaks first only if the unwind becomes a luxury-consumer downturn; then the multiple compresses toward a through-cycle luxury multiple; then the balance sheet — net debt €27.9bn — is comfortable. Watch: organic growth returning to 3–4%, FX drag (about €1bn of EBIT in 2026 in the pack's data), and Chinese demand.
