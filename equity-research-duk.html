AT A GLANCE

The investment case in one view

Duke is a pure regulated-utility compounder: earnings grow when invested capital (rate base) grows and regulators allow a return on it. The company plan is unusually large — $103B of capex over 2026–2030, taking rate base to $120B by 2030 — and unusually exposed to a new variable: data-centre load. Agreements total 7.8 GW (5.2 GW under construction), with data centres expected to be ~25% of Carolinas demand by 2030 on the company plan as reported. That is the growth story. The constraint is equally clear in the cash flow: in 2025, operating cash flow did not cover capex plus the dividend, leaving a $4.97B funding gap met from debt and equity — every year of the plan.

01

Business Model & Segment Breakdown

Duke Energy is a fully-regulated electric and gas utility. It does not sell a product at a market price; it invests in generation, transmission and distribution assets inside franchised service territories, and state commissions set the rates that give it an allowed return on that invested capital (the rate base). Revenue quality is therefore high and recurring, growth is capex-led, and the binding constraint is regulatory — every major earnings assumption in this note ultimately reduces to will commissions approve the spend and the tariff that recovers it?

The growth engine — rate base, not volume pricing

  • $103B capital plan, 2026–2030 — described in the pack as the largest fully-regulated capex plan in the industry (company plan, reported Oct 2026; confirmed via Zacks). Source: AInvest · Zacks verification.
  • Rate base to $120B by 2030 on that plan — rate-base growth is the mechanical driver of regulated earnings: more approved capital in service, at an allowed return, is more EPS.
  • Earnings growth of 9.6% p.a. through the decade is the figure attached to the plan in the same reporting — treat it as the plan's ambition, above the company's own 5–7% EPS growth guide through 2030 that accompanies the $6.55–$6.80 2026 guidance (Zacks). The gap between 5–7% guided and 9.6% plan-implied is exactly the regulatory-execution risk priced in §5.
  • Data-centre energy-service agreements: 7.8 GW total, 5.2 GW under construction. This is contracted / in-build load, not a pipeline of enquiries — but converting it to earnings still requires the tariffs and interconnections in §4.

Structural pivot — from steady utility to load-growth utility

  • Load growth is back. After two decades of flat US power demand, data-centre demand makes Duke a growth utility again — if, and only if, the “load creator pays” principle in new law (FL SB 484) lets Duke serve that load without shifting costs onto existing residential customers in a way commissions will not accept.
  • Scale of the queue vs. scale of what can be served: in Florida, only ~55 MW is servable before settlement expiry against a smallest study request of 160 MW, with 13 requests above 50 MW (MGrid). The demand is real; the near-term servable capacity is the bottleneck.
  • Supply-chain dependencies: as a regulated builder, Duke's critical path is large transformers, switchgear, gas turbines / generation equipment, and grid-interconnection capacity — long-lead electrical equipment is the industry-wide constraint on converting capex plans into in-service rate base on schedule. Delays do not cancel earnings, they defer them — but deferral at this funding cost matters (see §2).

Financial Health & Balance Sheet Diagnostics

The P&L looks like a textbook utility: mid-teens forward multiple, guided EPS growth, a rising dividend. The cash-flow statement is where this name differs from a sleepy compounder. Duke outspends its operating cash flow by design for the whole plan period — which makes it, in effect, a continuous issuer of debt and equity whose earnings per share depend on issuing that equity at a decent price and on commissions keeping allowed returns ahead of funding costs.

The one number to watch — the funding gap, every year

The pack's own headline is the diagnosis: the $103B spending plan outpaces Duke's cash flow every year. That is normal for a growing utility in any single year; as a five-year structure it means: (1) ongoing equity issuance dilutes the EPS growth the capex is meant to create; (2) rising rates directly tax the investment case, because the spread between allowed return and funding cost is the profit; and (3) the dividend is funded, in cash terms, partly by capital markets — sustainable while markets stay open and the stock holds its multiple, fragile if either closes. None of this is a solvency warning. It is a cost-of-capital warning, and it is why §6 holds short-term rather than buys aggressively here.

Capital allocation, in order of current priority

  • 1. Rate-base capex — $103B over five years dwarfs everything else; it is the strategy.
  • 2. Dividend — $3.3B/yr and growing slowly (+1.9% on the latest declaration): the income contract with the shareholder base is maintained, but growth in the payout is deliberately modest while capex peaks.
  • 3. External funding as the balancing item — debt first, equity as needed. Watch the equity leg: issuance below book or at a compressed multiple converts rate-base growth into flat EPS, which is the bear case in §5.
  • Buybacks — not a feature of a company with a structural funding gap; none is assumed in any scenario here.
03

Competitive Moat & Industry Positioning

Moat rating: wide but borrowed — the moat is the franchise, and the franchise is granted by regulators. The pack puts it in one line: “No pricing power beyond regulators — the moat is the rate case.” Inside its territories Duke faces no direct competitor for wires and regulated generation; outside them, it competes for capital, for data-centre siting, and for regulatory goodwill against every other Southeast utility chasing the same load.

Moat strengths

  • Franchised territories: Carolinas, Florida, Indiana, Ohio, Kentucky — a legal monopoly on regulated service in each. A rival cannot overbuild Duke's wires; entry is by commission grant, not by investment.
  • Scale: $99.33B market cap, #3 in the XLU top 10 (TipRanks, 30 Oct) behind NEE and SO — scale spreads fixed regulatory, financing and procurement costs over a $120B-by-2030 rate base.
  • Data-centre queue position: 7.8 GW of energy-service agreements (5.2 GW under construction) in the Carolinas/Florida corridor — hyperscalers are queuing for Duke's territories (power availability, land, Southeast siting), and that queue is itself a scarce asset.
  • Revenue visibility: regulated returns on approved capital are among the most forecastable earnings streams in the market — hence 5–7% guided EPS growth through 2030 can be stated at all.

Moat limits & competitors

  • No pricing power: prices are set in rate cases. Cost inflation, storm costs or funding costs that commissions will not pass through come straight out of earned return.
  • The moat is revocable at the margin: the Florida PSC staff recommendation to deny the large-load tariff as filed (§4) is the moat's limit made concrete — Duke can be told to refile the very instrument its growth plan needs.
  • Peer competition for load & capital: NextEra (FPL in Florida), Southern Company, Dominion, AEP and Constellation all compete for the same data-centre siting decisions and the same utility-investor dollar; Duke has no structural edge over NEE in Florida itself.
  • Political exposure: residential bill pressure is the binding political constraint on every rate case — “load creator pays” law (SB 484) exists precisely because commissions must be seen to protect existing customers from data-centre costs.

Positioning verdict: Duke is the pure-play regulated growth utility in the top tier — unlike NextEra (large unregulated renewables arm) or Constellation (merchant nuclear), essentially all of the thesis is regulated rate-base compounding. That purity is the attraction for an income/defensive sleeve and the limitation for upside: there is no unregulated earnings kicker, only execution on approvals.

04

Catalysts & Downside Risks

Immediate event — Florida PSC vote, 3 Nov 2026 (two days after this note)

Florida PSC staff on 22 Oct 2026 recommended commissioners DENY Duke Florida's large-load tariff as filed, with leave to refile — a staff recommendation, not a commission decision as of 1 Nov. The commission vote is scheduled 3 Nov 2026 (Docket 20260064-EI). This is the first tariff filed under FL SB 484 (effective 1 Jul 2026: “load creator pays”, 50 MW threshold). Context that decides how much it matters: only ~55 MW is servable before settlement expiry vs. a smallest study request of 160 MW, with 13 requests >50 MW. A denial-as-filed with a workable refile path is a delay; a denial that strands the queue is a thesis event. Do not pre-position at full size ahead of it. Source: MGrid, 24 Oct 2026.

Catalysts (12–18 months)

  • Florida tariff resolution — approval, or a refiled tariff that passes, unblocks the Florida leg of the load-growth story and validates the SB 484 framework for the whole state.
  • Q3 2026 results, 5 Nov 2026 — first print after this note; the read is not the quarter itself but any update to the $103B plan's funding mix and to 2026 guidance ($6.55–$6.80).
  • Rate-base conversion — 5.2 GW already under construction moving into service is the mechanical EPS escalator toward the $120B-by-2030 rate base.
  • Carolinas load delivery — data centres reaching ~25% of Carolinas demand by 2030 (plan) would make Duke's 5–7% guide look conservative and support the bull case's 7% growth.
  • Rate relief / falling funding costs — any decline in long rates widens the allowed-return-minus-funding-cost spread and re-rates the whole regulated group; DUK is a geared play on that spread.

Downside risks

  • Regulatory denial / delay (live): the Florida staff recommendation is the template risk — each delayed tariff defers in-service dates and pushes earnings right while funding costs run now.
  • Funding gap + dilution: a $4.97B gap in 2025, widening on the plan's own capex ramp (≈$20.6B/yr average vs. $14.02B in 2025). Persistent equity issuance at a soft price is the slow bear case: rate base grows, EPS doesn't.
  • Interest-rate exposure: utilities are the market's bond proxy. A higher-for-longer long end compresses the multiple (19.1× trailing has room to fall) and raises the cost of the debt leg simultaneously — a double hit few other sectors face.
  • Execution on $103B: long-lead equipment, interconnection queues and the 55 MW-servable constraint in Florida all say the same thing — announced capex is not in-service capex until commissions and construction say so.
  • Storm & climate costs: a Florida/Carolinas service territory carries hurricane restoration exposure that lands in rate cases with a lag and political friction attached. (General territory risk — no specific storm figure is carried in the verified pack.)
  • Data-centre demand concentration: if hyperscaler build plans slow, Duke is left having pre-funded grid capacity for load that arrives late — the mirror image of the bull case, and the reason the “load creator pays” allocation matters so much.
What would break the thesis

A pattern, not a headline: two or more major-jurisdiction tariff/rate outcomes in a row that push load costs onto Duke's balance sheet or strand the large-load queue, while the funding gap forces equity issuance at a falling multiple. Either leg alone is the ordinary friction of regulated utilities. Together they turn 5–7% guided EPS growth into the bear case's 4.5% — growth that barely covers dilution.

Methodology note — read first. Every price in this section is a modelled estimate, not a verified fact, and is labelled as such per the verification protocol for this series: the independent verifier could not recompute weighted targets where scenario weights were not in the pack, so the weights used here are stated explicitly (Bear 25% / Base 50% / Bull 25% — the series convention) so the arithmetic can be checked. For a regulated utility the frozen-parameter model is run in its EPS form: 2031 price = current EPS × (1 + g)5 × exit P/E, with EPS growth g and the exit P/E frozen per case. This is the transparent adaptation of the standard “2031 revenue × net margin ÷ shares × P/E” format — for Duke, EPS growth is the rate-base-driven variable (revenue × margin ÷ shares collapses to EPS), and starting EPS is anchored to guidance: the pack's model base is $6.67, against an arithmetic guidance midpoint of $6.675 for the $6.55–$6.80 range.

Recommendation & Portfolio Sizing

Investor profile assumed, as specified: mid-30s, long accumulation horizon, self-rated risk tolerance 8/10. For this profile a regulated utility is not where return is manufactured — it is where equity risk is diluted: low correlation to the AI/tech core of the wider coverage set, a contractual-looking dividend, and earnings that do not depend on the ad cycle or the chip cycle. Size it as ballast, and it earns its place; size it as a growth name, and it will disappoint by design.

Sizing for this profile

  • DUK target: 2.5% of total portfolio.   • Income maths at target weight: 2.5% × ~3.4% yield ≈ 0.09% of portfolio value per year from DUK dividends alone — small in isolation, but it is the most contract-like 0.09% in the book.

DUK — Customer base (B2C-like note — no top-7 business-customer list)

Duke sells electricity and gas principally to households and small businesses through regulated Carolinas, Florida, Indiana, Ohio and Kentucky tariffs, so it is treated as B2C-like for this task and no top-7 business-customer list is given. The pack discloses data-centre energy-service agreements totalling 7.8 GW (5.2 GW under construction) and data centres at ~25% of Carolinas demand by 2030, but names no individual data-centre customer, so no customer table is built.

Key demand drivers: rate-base growth toward $120B by 2030 on the $103B five-year plan, the Florida PSC's 3 Nov 2026 vote on Duke Florida's large-load tariff (staff recommended denial as filed on 22 Oct 2026), and data-centre load materialisation in the Carolinas.

DUK — AI trade positioning

Role: Beneficiary. Duke has the largest fully-regulated capex plan in the industry at $103B over five years, with data-centre agreements of 7.8 GW (5.2 GW under construction) and data centres expected at ~25% of Carolinas demand by 2030.

While the AI trade runs — demand channel: Contracted data-centre load converts into rate-base growth toward $120B by 2030 and the pack's 9.6% p.a. earnings growth, since regulated earnings scale with approved rate base.

While the AI trade runs — multiple channel: Duke's multiple (forward P/E 15.95 per pack, the lowest among the large regulated names here) already prices funding scepticism rather than AI euphoria, so the multiple channel is modest upside if the tariff and funding questions clear.

When the AI trade unwinds — what breaks, in order: First, demand: the Florida PSC vote on 3 Nov 2026 — staff recommended denial of Duke Florida's large-load tariff as filed — and any Carolinas slippage in the 7.8 GW queue, stranding part of the $103B plan. Second, multiple compression: limited, since the multiple is already low, but a failed tariff refile would cap re-rating. Third, balance sheet: this is where it bites — the pack flags a $4.97B 2025 funding gap, so an AI unwind that raises funding costs while load disappoints forces more dilutive equity. No quantified sensitivity is claimed.

Trigger(s) to watch: The 3 Nov 2026 Florida PSC vote and any refile; Q3 results due 5 Nov 2026 (post-cutoff, not used here); and Carolinas data-centre agreement conversions from the 7.8 GW queue.
