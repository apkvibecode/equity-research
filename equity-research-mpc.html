§1

Business Model & Segment Breakdown

What MPC actually is

Marathon Petroleum is the largest US refiner by capacity, a position created by the 2018 Andeavor acquisition. The model is downstream-led: buy crude, refine it into gasoline, diesel and other products, and sell into wholesale and retail channels — capturing the crack spread (the gap between crude input costs and refined-product prices) rather than the crude price itself. That distinction drives everything in this note: MPC earnings rise when refining margins widen, which is exactly what powered the +173% YTD move — the pack is explicit that the run is crack-spread driven, not a low-base effect.

Revenue streams

  • Refining & marketing (core). The dominant stream inside TTM revenue of $153.6bn. Earnings here are spread-driven and volatile by construction — a wide crack is a tailwind this year and a mean-reversion risk next year.
  • Retail. 7,700+ stations give MPC a branded and franchised outlet for its own product, smoothing some wholesale volatility and anchoring demand for refinery output.
  • Midstream via MPLX. MPC holds ~64% of MPLX, its midstream partnership. Pipelines, logistics and gathering assets earn fee-based income that is far steadier than refining margins, and MPLX distributions are a recurring cash channel back to MPC.

Competitive Moat & Industry Positioning

Where the moat is real

  • Refining scale. Largest US refiner by capacity. Scale buys crude-slate flexibility, procurement leverage and the ability to run complex barrels competitors cannot process as profitably.
  • Permitting barrier. New US refining capacity is effectively not being built — the existing fleet, MPC's included, is a scarce, hard-to-replicate asset base. Incumbent capacity is the barrier to entry.
  • Midstream fee income via MPLX. The ~64% MPLX stake adds contracted, fee-based logistics earnings that damp the refining cycle — a structural cushion pure refiners lack.
  • Retail footprint. 7,700+ stations secure an outlet for refined product and a consumer-facing brand layer on top of a commodity business.

Where the moat stops

  • No pricing power over the product. Gasoline and diesel are commodities priced off benchmarks. MPC's edge is cost, scale and logistics — it cannot set the crack spread; it can only capture it efficiently.
  • Margin is market-given. The same openness that produced +173% YTD works in reverse: refining margins are set by industry capacity, demand and crude differentials, all outside management control.
  • Competitive set. Other large US refiners and integrated majors with refining arms compete for the same crude slates and product markets; MPC's defence is its capacity position and system integration, not differentiation.
  • Regulatory exposure as anti-moat. Renewable-fuel obligations (RINs) and environmental regulation are recurring cost lines for refiners — flagged in the pack as a standing risk, not a one-off.

Moat framing per the research pack: “Refining scale, midstream fee income via MPLX, logistics.”

§4

Catalysts & Downside Risks

Catalysts

  • Crack spreads staying wide. The engine of the +173% run — every extra quarter of strong refining margins extends the earnings base the 14.6× multiple sits on.
  • Q3 print, 3 Nov 2026. The first hard test of whether current margins are holding; due two days after this note.
  • Dividend momentum. The 10% raise to $1.10/quarter (payable 10 Dec) signals management's confidence and supports the income case at ~1.0% yield.
  • MPLX distributions. Growth in fee-based midstream cash flows raises the non-cyclical share of MPC's income.
  • Buybacks. Flagged in the pack as an ongoing return channel alongside the dividend.

Downside risks

  • Refining-margin mean reversion — the dominant risk. After +173% YTD, the stock prices in continued strong cracks. Normalisation toward mid-cycle margins compresses earnings and the price together; the pack names this the principal risk.
  • RINs and regulation. Renewable-fuel credit costs and environmental rules can move refiner economics quickly and are set by policy, not markets.
  • Demand erosion. Fuel demand is in long-run structural decline pressure (efficiency, electrification); a soft demand year plus new competing supply would squeeze the spread from both sides.
  • Cyclical multiple trap. 14.6× looks cheap, but on peak-cycle earnings a “cheap” P/E can be the most expensive kind — the E in the ratio falls before the P adjusts.
  • Concentration in one variable. Unlike an integrated major, MPC has no upstream earnings to offset a refining downturn; MPLX cushions but does not hedge the core.

AI trade positioning — Marathon Petroleum (MPC)

Role: Low exposure. Refining and transport fuels have only a thin AI channel — backup diesel for data centres and construction fuel — and Marathon's earnings are set by crack spreads, not by AI capex.

While the AI trade runs — demand channel: While the AI trade runs, demand barely moves: data centres buy backup diesel in small volumes and construction fuel in passing, while Marathon's earnings are set by crack spreads on gasoline, diesel and jet fuel.

While the AI trade runs — multiple channel: On multiples, there is no AI premium to unwind (trailing P/E 14.6 in the pack; the +173% YTD run is a crack-spread story, not AI), so compression would come from refining-margin normalisation.

When the AI trade unwinds, what breaks in order: (1) demand — hyperscaler capex cuts, data-centre delays/cancellations, and the commodity-price leg; (2) multiple compression toward a pre-AI/through-cycle multiple; (3) balance sheet — On the balance sheet, there is no AI-capex debt: the risk is working-capital/inventory swings if product demand falls.

Specific trigger(s) to watch: Triggers to watch: crack-spread collapse, a transport-fuel demand downturn, and RIN/regulatory changes. No quantified AI sensitivity is claimed (no pack figure supports one). Key distinction: commodity prices, not AI sentiment, drive this name, so an AI unwind hits it mainly through the commodity cycle and risk appetite.
