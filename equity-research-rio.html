Rio Tinto (NYSE: RIO)

The earnings engine has quietly flipped. In H1 2026 copper — not iron ore — carried the growth: copper EBITDA rose 84% while iron ore EBITDA slipped 1%, and group net earnings jumped 47%. Rio is still priced like a pure iron-ore annuity at a low-teens earnings multiple on the pack's figures, but the portfolio underneath it is becoming a copper-and-energy-transition miner, with Simandou and Oyu Tolgoi as the two big volume stories into 2028.

Business Model & Segment Breakdown

Rio Tinto is a diversified miner operating across 34 countries, but its profit pool has historically been dominated by one asset system: the Pilbara iron ore complex in Western Australia. The investment case in 2026 is that this is changing — copper is scaling fast, aluminium and lithium add energy-transition exposure, and Simandou in Guinea will add a second world-scale iron ore province. Rio sells commodities at market prices; it does not set them. Volume, cost position and portfolio mix are the only levers it controls.

Iron ore and copper together are $12.5B of the $14.83B H1 underlying EBITDA — about 84%. Everything else (aluminium, lithium, other minerals, corporate) is the ≈$2.3B combined residual. H1 2026, verified figures.

The four commodity legs

  • Iron ore — Pilbara: the cash engine. Scale and low unit costs keep it profitable through the cycle, but H1 EBITDA was flat (−1%) — the segment now funds the transition rather than driving growth.
  • Copper: the growth engine. EBITDA +84% in H1 on the Oyu Tolgoi underground ramp in Mongolia; copper is the metal most directly levered to electrification demand.
  • Aluminium: part of the residual above; a steadier, more downstream-linked business that diversifies the earnings mix away from bulk commodities.
  • Lithium: the option leg — strategically aligned with batteries, but earnings are hostage to a volatile lithium price (see §4).

Structural pivots (2026–28)

  • Copper / aluminium / lithium pivot: capital and growth are being re-pointed from pure iron ore toward energy-transition metals — H1's EBITDA mix is the first print where that pivot is visible in the numbers, not just the strategy slides.
  • Simandou (Guinea): a 60 Mtpa iron ore project targeting H2 2028 — the largest new iron ore supply source in a generation, and Rio's second iron ore province. It diversifies geography but adds Guinea execution and political risk.
  • Productivity: a $1.8B run-rate productivity target — cost-out that, if delivered, drops almost entirely to EBITDA in a price-taker business.

Geographic footprint & dependencies

  • Operations in 34 countries, with the profit core concentrated in Australia (Pilbara iron ore), Mongolia (Oyu Tolgoi copper) and, from 2028, Guinea (Simandou). A geographic revenue split was not in the verified pack and is not estimated here.
  • Demand dependency: iron ore economics are set by seaborne demand — overwhelmingly Asian steel production — and by the benchmark iron ore price, over which Rio has no control. A sustained fall in that price is the single largest earnings sensitivity (§4).
  • Supply-chain dependencies: large-scale mining is energy-, diesel-, rail- and port-intensive; Pilbara's integrated rail/port system is itself part of the moat (§3), while Simandou requires new rail and port infrastructure in Guinea — a build dependency as much as a mining one.

03

Competitive Moat & Industry Positioning

Moat rating: wide on cost and scale, narrow on price. Mining moats are not brands or networks — they are ore bodies, cost curves and infrastructure. Rio's moat is that its core assets sit low on the global cost curve and cannot be replicated: nobody can build another Pilbara. What Rio does not have is pricing power — it is a price-taker in every commodity it sells, which is why the multiple in §2 sits in the low teens and not at 25×.

Moat strengths

  • Pilbara scale and cost position: one of the world's largest, lowest-cost iron ore systems, with integrated rail and port infrastructure that smaller rivals cannot economically duplicate.
  • Tier-1 ore bodies: asset quality — grade, scale, mine life — is the real barrier to entry in mining, and Rio's portfolio (Pilbara, Oyu Tolgoi) is built on tier-1 deposits.
  • Oyu Tolgoi copper growth: a large, long-life copper asset already ramping — in a metal where new supply is scarce and slow, owning growth that is already built is a genuine advantage (H1 copper EBITDA +84%).
  • Simandou optionality: 60 Mtpa targeted from H2 2028 gives Rio a second iron ore province and high-grade supply that can displace higher-cost producers on the curve.
  • Diversification improving: copper, aluminium and lithium reduce the single-commodity dependence that has historically defined Rio's earnings volatility.

Moat limits & competitors

  • Price-taker economics: no pricing power in iron ore, copper, aluminium or lithium. Earnings quality is set by the cost curve position, not by the ability to raise prices.
  • BHP — the closest peer: comparable Pilbara scale, larger market cap ($292.4B as printed in the ranking source, unverified), competing for the same "quality diversified miner" allocation.
  • Vale and Fortescue in iron ore; Freeport-McMoRan and Southern Copper in copper — each with its own cost/grade position; share gains come from cost curves, not marketing.
  • Simandou cuts both ways: 60 Mtpa of new high-grade supply entering the seaborne market from 2028 pressures the iron ore price Rio itself receives — Rio is partly competing with its own future tonnes.
  • Resource nationalism: tier-1 assets sit in host countries (Mongolia, Guinea) whose governments are counterparties, not bystanders — fiscal and permitting terms can move.

Positioning verdict: Rio is the diversified-miner expression of two trades at once — Pilbara cash generation today, copper/Simandou volume growth into 2028. Its moat protects margins through the cycle, not the earnings level in any given year; that distinction is the whole reason for the conservative sizing in §6.

04

Catalysts & Downside Risks

Catalysts (12–18 months and beyond)

  • Copper momentum compounding — if H1's +84% copper EBITDA trajectory holds as Oyu Tolgoi ramps, the mix shift re-rates what "Rio earnings" means: less iron-ore beta, more electrification beta.
  • Simandou milestones — construction and infrastructure progress toward the 60 Mtpa, H2 2028 target; each de-risking step converts a promised volume story into a bankable one.
  • Productivity delivery — progress toward the $1.8B run-rate target is pure margin in a price-taker business and within management's control, unlike prices.
  • Dividend trajectory — after a +43% interim raise, the full-year declaration is the next cash-return signal; sustained FCF growth funds it without leverage.
  • Aluminium / lithium pivot — further evidence that the non-iron-ore portfolio can carry group growth in a flat iron ore year, as copper did in H1.

Downside risks

  • Iron ore price — still the largest single EBITDA contributor ($6.8B in H1). A sustained price fall flows almost straight to earnings; there is no hedge in the business model against it.
  • Guinea execution — Simandou means building mine, rail and port in Guinea on a fixed narrative timeline (H2 2028). Slippage, cost overrun or political disruption delays the entire second-province thesis.
  • Safety — two fatalities in H1 were flagged in the source reporting. Beyond the human cost, safety failures drive regulatory scrutiny, stoppages and reputational damage; this is an open item to monitor, not a closed one.
  • Lithium price — the lithium leg's earnings are hostage to one of the most volatile prices in commodities; a prolonged lithium downturn makes that part of the pivot a drag.
  • China-linked demand cycle — seaborne iron ore demand is concentrated in Asian steel; a property/steel downturn there hits price and volume simultaneously.
  • Self-cannibalising supply — Simandou's 60 Mtpa arriving into the seaborne market from 2028 can soften the very price Rio's Pilbara tonnes earn.
What would break the thesis

A sustained iron ore price decline while Simandou slips beyond 2028. Either alone is survivable — copper growth offsets a soft iron ore year, and the balance sheet absorbs a project delay. Together they remove both the current cash engine's price support and the future volume story, leaving a flat, fully price-exposed miner at a multiple that assumed growth.

AI trade positioning — Rio Tinto (ADR) (RIO)

Role: Beneficiary. Rio's copper growth (EBITDA +84% in H1 2026, Oyu Tolgoi) is its AI-electrification channel, while its larger iron-ore business rides Chinese steel demand, not AI — so the AI beta is real but partial.

While the AI trade runs — demand channel: While the AI trade runs, demand comes through electrification: data centres need grid connections, transformers and wiring, all copper-intensive, and the grid build-out behind AI is a multi-year copper demand leg.

While the AI trade runs — multiple channel: On multiples, the AI premium here is really a copper premium (trailing P/E 11.9 in the pack where verified): an unwind would compress it toward a through-cycle mining multiple as copper prices fall.

When the AI trade unwinds, what breaks in order: (1) demand — hyperscaler capex cuts, data-centre delays/cancellations, and the commodity-price leg; (2) multiple compression toward a pre-AI/through-cycle multiple; (3) balance sheet — On the balance sheet, there is no AI-capex debt: the risk is growth capex committed at peak copper prices that becomes uneconomic if copper falls.

Specific trigger(s) to watch: Triggers to watch: copper-price reversal, Chinese construction/property weakness, hyperscaler capex cuts or delays, and mine-permit or social-licence setbacks. No quantified AI sensitivity is claimed (no pack figure supports one). Key distinction: commodity prices, not AI sentiment, drive this name, so an AI unwind hits it mainly through the commodity cycle and risk appetite.
