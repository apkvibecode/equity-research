01

Business model & segment breakdown

Rent + royalties first, company restaurants second

McDonald's earns money three ways, and the order matters: (1) rent from franchisees on land and buildings McDonald's often owns, (2) royalties as a percentage of franchisee sales, and (3) sales from company-operated restaurants — the smallest, lowest-margin piece at roughly 5% of locations.

Because reported revenue (~$27B, ESTIMATE) captures only rent, royalties and company-store sales, it understates the system: total system-wide sales across all franchised stores are far larger. That is why MCD's operating margin looks unusually high for a restaurant company.

  • Global footprint, US largest — the US is the biggest market, with a large international franchised base providing diversification.
  • Refranchising pivot — the long-running shift toward franchised ownership reduces capital intensity and earnings volatility.
  • Real estate at the core — ownership / control of prime sites is both a revenue line (rent) and a barrier competitors cannot quickly copy.
  • Digital & loyalty — app, loyalty programme and delivery are the main growth levers for frequency and ticket size.
  • Menu growth areas — chicken and coffee are highlighted growth categories alongside core burgers.
  • Supply-chain dependency — franchisee health, food and labour costs, and consistent supply across thousands of independent operators.

At 21× earnings MCD is not cheap in absolute terms, but it is priced for what it is: a high-margin, franchised cash generator rather than a cyclical restaurant operator. The balance-sheet trade-off is deliberate — the company has historically carried high debt due to buybacks, returning cash to shareholders while relying on the stability of rent and royalty income to service it. That works while franchisee sales hold up; it amplifies downside if system sales soften.

Capital allocation in one line: fund the system and digital/loyalty investment, pay a dividend (~2.5% yield, estimate), and return the rest via buybacks — the source of the elevated leverage noted above. 03

Competitive moat & positioning

Brand + scale + real estate + the franchisee system itself

  • Brand: one of the most recognised consumer brands globally — pricing power is real but bounded by its value promise (see Risks).
  • Scale: purchasing, advertising and technology costs spread over a vast system no single rival matches in QSR burgers.
  • Real estate: control of prime locations converts a restaurant business into a property-backed royalty stream.
  • Franchisee system: thousands of aligned owner-operators provide local execution and capital — and are hard to replicate.
  • Yum! Brands (KFC, Taco Bell, Pizza Hut) — global franchised scale rival.
  • Restaurant Brands International (Burger King, Tim Hortons, Popeyes) — closest burger-franchise comparator.
  • Starbucks — competes for the coffee / daytime occasion.
  • Wendy's and local QSR chains — direct burger and value-menu competition market by market.
04

Catalysts & downside risks

Value perception is both the engine and the vulnerability

  • Value menu — a credible value offer can rebuild traffic among price-sensitive customers.
  • Loyalty & digital — higher frequency, better data, and delivery growth.
  • International growth — continued expansion and refranchising in international markets.
  • Chicken & coffee — category growth beyond the core burger occasion.
  • Consumer value pressure — if customers judge MCD as no longer cheap, traffic falls quickly.
  • Labour & food costs — squeeze franchisee margins first, then system health.
  • Franchisee relations — rent/royalty tension rises when store-level economics weaken.
  • Health trends — long-run shift in eating habits and regulation around fast food.

Customers — MCD (B2C note)

Consumer-facing: no top-7 business-customer list. Revenue is franchise rent and royalties plus company-operated sales, ultimately paid by individual diners, and franchisees are a fragmented operator base rather than a concentrated customer set disclosed above 10%. Key demand drivers: US and international comparable sales, value-menu and digital/loyalty uptake, and franchisee economics.

AI trade positioning — MCD

Role: Low exposure. McDonald's AI exposure is digital, loyalty and restaurant automation, not infrastructure: the pack's thesis is franchised rent and royalties, digital/loyalty and delivery, with FCF of $7.8bn TTM.

While the AI trade runs. While the AI trade runs, demand grows modestly through digital ordering, loyalty and drive-thru automation lifting throughput; the multiple is supported by franchise economics, not an AI premium.

When the AI trade unwinds. If the trade unwinds, demand breaks first only if the unwind becomes a consumer downturn hitting traffic; then the multiple compresses toward a through-cycle restaurant multiple; then the balance sheet — debt $54.6bn and negative book equity from buybacks — limits buyback flexibility. Watch: US comparable sales (only 0.8% in the pack's latest data point), franchisee economics, and the $8.5bn franchisee-support commitment.
