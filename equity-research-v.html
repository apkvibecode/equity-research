1.Business model & segment breakdown

Visa sells the rails, not the loans. Issuing banks carry the credit risk on card balances; Visa's economics come from connecting those banks, merchants, and cardholders — and charging for each leg of the connection.

How the money is made

  • Service revenues — fees tied to payments volume on Visa-branded cards.
  • Data-processing revenues — per-transaction fees for authorisation, clearing and settlement.
  • International-transaction revenues — cross-border and currency-conversion fees; the highest-value leg, and the one growing fastest (below).
  • Client incentives — payments back to banks and partners to win and keep volume. This is the counterweight to watch: incentives were $4.68bn in fiscal Q3 2026, and Q3 operating expenses rose 19%, outpacing revenue growth.

Scale & reach

  • Accepted in 200+ countries; 258bn transactions in FY2025 (Fool).
  • TTM revenue $44.5bn with gross profit of $35.7bn (Fool) — a gross margin of roughly 80%.
  • Fiscal Q4 2026 (reported 27 Oct 2026): 74.2bn transactions, +10%.

Dependencies

  • Clients: banks and fintechs issue the cards and bring the volume — Visa depends on keeping them incentivised.
  • Regulators: interchange fees are politically and legally contested (see §4).
  • Travel: cross-border volume leans on international travel and spending.

3.Competitive moat & industry positioning

What protects the toll

  • Two-sided network: cardholders go where merchants accept, merchants accept what cardholders carry — a self-reinforcing loop across 200+ countries.
  • Brand and trust: acceptance is the product; a failed or declined transaction is the one outcome the network sells protection against.
  • Fraud, AI and tokenisation investment: security tooling and tokenised credentials raise the cost for a rival rail to be seen as equally safe.
  • Pricing power: evidenced by the model itself — roughly 80% gross margin on TTM figures, sustained at enormous transaction scale.

Who is trying to route around it

  • Mastercard — the direct global-network rival, facing the same interchange settlement (§4).
  • American Express — a closed-loop competitor with its own issuing economics.
  • PayPal — a digital-wallet layer that can sit on top of, or steer volume away from, card rails.
  • Local rails — UPI, Pix and similar national real-time systems, which move money account-to-account without a card network in the middle.
  • Stablecoin rails — an emerging cross-border alternative aimed at Visa's most profitable leg.

The moat is real but it is a regulatory moat as much as a technological one: acceptance and trust are hard to replicate, yet the price of the toll — interchange — is set in courtrooms and legislatures as much as in the market. That is why §4 leads with litigation.

4.Catalysts & downside risks

Catalysts

  • Cross-border & travel: cross-border volume grew +13% (fiscal Q3) and +14% (fiscal Q4) — the richest revenue leg is also the fastest-growing.
  • Value-added services: selling fraud, data and advisory services on top of the rails.
  • Tokenisation: deeper token adoption embeds Visa credentials inside phones, wallets and checkouts.
  • Real-time payments: participation in instant-payment flows rather than ceding them entirely to local rails.

Risks

  • Interchange litigation: Visa booked a $469m provision in fiscal Q4 for the interchange (MDL) case — confirmed in its earnings release.
  • The settlement hearing — 16 Nov 2026: a final-approval hearing before Judge Cogan on a settlement that would let ~12 million US merchants decline premium/commercial cards, cut credit interchange by 10bp for 5 years, and cap standard cards at 1.25% for 8 years.
  • Regulation of interchange and network rules more broadly.
  • Disintermediation: CBDCs and local rails (UPI, Pix) routing volume off card networks.
  • Consumer-spending slowdown: a toll on spending falls when spending does.
  • Cost creep: fiscal Q3 opex +19% vs revenue +14%, with client incentives at $4.68bn in the quarter.

Wording matters on the hearing: it is a final-approval hearing for the settlement — not a standalone ruling “on refusal” — and the merchant-refusal right only exists if the settlement is approved on those terms.

6.Recommendation & position sizing

Short term (12–18 mo) — Buy

The pack's call is Buy, anchored on the modelled $460 base target. The supporting evidence in the verified figures: two consecutive quarters of double-digit revenue and cross-border growth (fiscal Q3 +14% revenue, fiscal Q4 +13%). The counterweight is event risk — the final-approval hearing on 16 Nov 2026 and the $469m provision already booked — plus a full 33.82× multiple that leaves little room for a spending slowdown.

Long term (5 yr) — Buy

Also Buy in the pack. The 5-year base case ($666, modelled) rests on 10% revenue growth at a 51.2% net margin with the multiple held at today's 33.82× — demanding, but the model's engine is the network in §3 rather than multiple expansion. The bear case ($391) is only 5% below today's price, which is what a de-rating to 26× does even with growth intact.

Fair values are computed from unrounded growth rates and EPS; the inputs displayed are rounded, so multiplying the rounded figures shown may differ from the fair value by a few cents.

AI trade positioning — Visa (V)

Role: Enabler / Beneficiary. While the AI trade runs, Visa is an enabler of the AI economy: payments, tokenisation and value-added services (fraud, data, security) ride on AI-driven commerce, including agentic and cross-border transactions — cross-border volume rose 14% in fiscal Q4 2026 (pack) — and AI helps Visa’s own fraud and authorisation tools.

While the AI trade runs — multiple channel: The multiple channel is real but not purely AI: Visa trades at ~34x trailing earnings on a high-margin network model, so part of that multiple reflects durable network growth rather than an AI premium; an AI unwind would still compress it toward a through-cycle network multiple.

When the AI trade unwinds: If the AI trade unwinds, what breaks in order is: (1) demand — consumer and cross-border spending slows and AI-commerce volumes disappoint; (2) multiple — compression from the mid-30s toward a lower through-cycle level; (3) balance sheet — low direct risk, since Visa takes no card credit risk, though the $469m interchange provision and the November 2026 merchant-refusal hearing (pack) are separate overhangs. Triggers to watch: cross-border volume deceleration, incentive costs rising faster than revenue, and an adverse interchange outcome.
