01

Business Model & Segment Breakdown

Gilead is a Foster City, California biopharma operating in more than 35 countries, built on antivirals and now diversifying into oncology, liver disease and inflammation. Economically it remains one dominant franchise — HIV — with three smaller businesses at very different stages: liver disease growing fast off a new launch, oncology split between a rising antibody-drug conjugate and a shrinking cell-therapy unit, and a COVID product fading to irrelevance.

Ranking basis for this series

GILD sits in the Health Care top 10 on a US-domiciled-only basis — the FinanceCharts global sector list (last updated 30 Oct 2026) filtered to US-domiciled names, where Gilead ranks 8th (LLY, JNJ, ABBV, MRK, UNH, TMO, AMGN, GILD, ABT, PFE). Global ranks differ — Roche, Novartis, Genmab and AstraZeneca sit between US names globally — and Danaher (11th US) and Intuitive Surgical (12th US) are reserves, not top-10 members. The concentration is the story: three quarters of every sales dollar is HIV. Diversification is a 2027–28 story, not a 2026 fact — cell therapy, the most visible diversification attempt, shrank 18% in the quarter.

Geography — what is and isn't verifiable

  • The Q3 release does not publish a total US/International revenue split, so no precise total-company US % is quoted in this report.
  • The best verifiable proxy is HIV: Zacks' Q3 metrics table puts HIV–US at $5.26B against $6.25B of total HIV — roughly 84% US. Treat the overall business as US-heavy on that evidence.
  • That US weight cuts both ways: it is where pricing and share are strongest, and it is where the 2028 Medicare price-setting (§4) and US policy risk land.

Structural pivots (2026)

  • HIV treatment → HIV prevention as a second engine: the lenacapavir franchise now spans twice-yearly Yeztugo, a once-weekly oral in front of the FDA, and a once-yearly investigational version — backed by royalty-free licences covering 120 high-incidence countries and a PAHO access deal. Prevention nearly doubling in a year is the proof point.
  • A 2026 M&A spree to diversify away from HIV: Arcellx, Tubulis and Ouro Medicines acquired, plus the PepStar oral IL-23R deal (Q4 upfront expected) and a Vir T-cell-engager collaboration. The strategic logic is sound; the accounting cost is the ~$12B of acquired IPR&D dissected in §2.
  • $32B of US R&D/manufacturing investment through 2030, with a new Research Center as the first of three projects — explicitly framed as de-risking US R&D and manufacturing, which implies current ex-US exposure is material even though the Q3 release does not quantify it.

Supply-chain dependencies

  • Small-molecule / antiviral manufacturing scale — the HIV base business is a high-volume, high-margin chemistry operation; continuity of supply is the franchise.
  • Biologics and ADC capacity — Trodelvy, and the Tubulis platform behind it, depend on antibody-drug-conjugate manufacturing that is scarce industry-wide.
  • Personalised cell-therapy cold chain — Yescarta, Tecartus and the incoming anito-cel are made per-patient with vein-to-vein logistics; capacity and turnaround time are competitive variables, not back-office details.

Supply-chain dependencies are described qualitatively: the sources used do not quantify them, and no percentage exposure is asserted.

Where the cash went — the decline is explained, not operational

Cash + securities fell $10.6B → $6.4B during 2026 because of deliberate outflows: $11.3B of acquisitions, $2.8B of debt repaid, $3.1B of dividends and $1.1B of buybacks, partly offset by $11.2B of operating cash flow and $4.1B of net debt financing. The business did not consume the cash pile; management spent it — on diversification whose returns are not yet in the numbers.

Capital allocation, in order of current priority

  • 1. M&A for diversification — Arcellx, Tubulis, Ouro and PepStar in a single year; the bet is that bought pipelines de-risk the post-2030 HIV story.
  • 2. The dividend — a decade of growth, covered ~3.4× by TTM free cash flow on a payout basis; the commitment device for income holders.
  • 3. US capacity — $32B of R&D/manufacturing investment through 2030.
  • 4. Buybacks — continuing but throttled (~$1.1B in 9M); a re-acceleration would signal the deal phase is over.
03

Competitive Moat & Industry Positioning

Moat rating: wide in HIV, narrow-to-building elsewhere. Gilead's moat is the standard-of-care position of Biktarvy — a single-tablet regimen with a record 53% US treatment share and high switching inertia once a patient is stable — now extended by the lenacapavir platform into prevention, where dosing convenience (an injection twice a year, against a daily pill) is itself the barrier. The ~88% non-GAAP gross margin is what realised pricing power looks like; the 2028 Medicare cap in §4 is the first hard limit placed on it.

Moat strengths

  • Standard-of-care incumbency: Biktarvy is the first-line default; clinicians switch stable patients reluctantly, which makes share sticky in both directions.
  • Lenacapavir platform: twice-yearly PrEP approved, once-weekly oral and once-yearly versions in development — a dosing ladder no rival can replicate quickly; Yeztugo + Descovy already hold ~50% of PrEP.
  • Patent estate + settlements: core Biktarvy patents run to ~2033, and generic settlements push the earliest US generic launch to 1 Apr 2036 — no major loss of exclusivity until 2036 (§4 keeps the two dates distinct).
  • Scale and pipeline depth: 49 clinical programmes cited on the Q3 call, funded by $13.8B of annual free cash flow without touching the dividend.
  • Regulatory and manufacturing barriers: FDA approval pathways plus ADC and cell-therapy capacity are slow, expensive moats for any challenger to cross.

Moat limits & competitors

  • ViiV Healthcare (GSK/Pfizer/Shionogi) — the direct HIV rival: dolutegravir regimens (Dovato, Triumeq) and long-acting cabotegravir (Cabenuva/Apretude) attack exactly the convenience ground lenacapavir defends.
  • Merck and Janssen — doravirine and rilpivirine/darunavir regimens keep the treatment market contested at the margin.
  • Generic PrEP — off-patent FTC/TDF costs a small fraction of branded prevention list prices, pressuring the prevention franchise's economics from below.
  • Oncology is someone else's moat: AstraZeneca/Daiichi Sankyo's Datroway is a direct Trop-2 ADC rival to Trodelvy, with Enhertu and the Merck/BMS/J&J immunotherapy franchises around it; in cell therapy, BMS and J&J already out-position Gilead — the −18% quarter is the evidence.
  • Concentration is the anti-moat: at ~75% of sales, one franchise's pricing, safety or share shock is the equity story; in HCV, AbbVie remains the reference competitor.

Pricing-power verdict: high, but now capped in one channel — realised margins near 88% (non-GAAP) prove the power, while the Medicare Maximum Fair Price (§4) proves it is no longer unlimited. Barriers to entry: patents, regulatory approval and manufacturing scale, in that order. Technological edge: long-acting antiviral chemistry — genuinely differentiated, and the reason the prevention pivot is credible rather than aspirational.

04

Catalysts & Downside Risks

Catalysts

  • Anito-cel FDA decision — 23 Dec 2026 (Arcellx): 4th-line-plus relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma. The key near-term binary for a cell-therapy unit that just shrank 18%.
  • Once-weekly oral lenacapavir — FDA decision expected Feb 2027; once-yearly lenacapavir Phase 3 (PURPOSE-365) ongoing; Bixlenvo (bictegravir/lenacapavir single tablet) already approved Aug 2026 for virologically suppressed switch patients.
  • Yeztugo ramp: on track for ~$1B in 2026 with PrEP share still building — the clearest proof the prevention engine is real.
  • Pipeline breadth: emvistegrast Phase 2 ulcerative-colitis success with Phase 3 planned 2027 (plus Crohn's exploration); PepStar oral IL-23R entering Phase 1; Trodelvy label expansion in first-line metastatic TNBC (US + EU now across PD-L1 status); Livdelzi momentum in PBC.

Downside risks

  • Medicare price-setting (IRA) — the largest quantified risk: CMS set Biktarvy's Maximum Fair Price at $2,600 per 30-day supply — a Medicare price, ~38% below the Medicare-channel list price (not a 38% cut to all Biktarvy revenue) — effective 1 Jan 2028. Gilead estimates a ~$1.5B net 2028 Biktarvy revenue impact, including spillover to Medicaid, 340B and other US payers, primarily Medicare. Management still expects total HIV revenue, product sales and EPS to grow in 2028 vs 2027 — but that depends on Yeztugo, Trodelvy and Livdelzi offsetting it, and is not yet proven.
  • Patent cliff — distant but real: core Biktarvy patents run to ~2033; settlements delay US generic entry to 1 Apr 2036. The 2028 price cut proves realised price can fall well before patent expiry. Generic oral PrEP and payer restrictions (e.g. Florida ADAP) already pressure gross-to-net — a 41% gross-to-net deduction rate was cited in a 10-Q-based analysis.
  • M&A execution: ~$12.4–12.9B of 2026 acquired IPR&D (guidance-table vs call basis, §2) plus debt-funded deals consumed the cash pile; further deals could strain leverage or dilute focus.
  • MFN and tariffs: Gilead has signed a US-government MFN agreement (19 Dec 2025) — Medicaid MFN discounts including HIV/HCV/HBV/COVID products, parity pricing on future launches, Epclusa sold direct-to-patient via TrumpRx, and a 3-year Section 232 tariff exemption conditional on further US manufacturing investment. The exemption mitigates tariff exposure, but no quantified Gilead-specific tariff figure was found in the sources used — that narrow point is unverified, and zero impact should not be assumed. Ex-US pricing (EU/UK health-technology assessments) and FX remain live exposures on a US-heavy but global business.
What would break the thesis

The 2028 offset failing in public: if, during 2027, Yeztugo/Trodelvy/Livdelzi growth visibly cannot cover the ~$1.5B net Biktarvy MFP hit while cell therapy keeps shrinking, the market stops capitalising "diversification" and re-prices Gilead as a single-franchise HIV name heading into a price cut — the bear case in §5. Conversely, one clean anito-cel approval (23 Dec 2026) plus a weekly-oral lenacapavir approval (Feb 2027) would make the offset arithmetic much easier to believe.
